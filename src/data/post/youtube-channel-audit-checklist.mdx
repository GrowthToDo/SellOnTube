## What is a YouTube channel audit for a business channel?

**A YouTube channel audit** is a structured review of a channel's videos, packaging and data that finds what stops the channel from reaching its goal. For a business channel, that goal is customer acquisition, so the audit measures leads and buyer reach, not audience growth.

Most audit guides you will find are written for creators. They check subscriber growth, average view duration and thumbnail CTR, then tell you to post more often. Those checks still matter, but they answer a creator's question: "how do I get more views?"

A business audit answers a different question: can this channel produce customers?

A YouTube channel audit for a business channel checks whether the channel can produce customers, in four parts. First, score the public metadata of your last 10 videos: do titles match what buyers search, do descriptions give a reason and a link to act, is publishing consistent, are chapters and playlists in place? Second, check every next-step surface: the channel page, pinned comments, end screens and the landing page each video sends people to. Third, read YouTube Studio for buyer signals: how much traffic comes from YouTube search, which search terms bring viewers, and whether the audience sits in the markets you sell to. Fourth, trace leads back to videos with tagged links, GA4 and a "how did you hear about us" field. Fix the weakest part first, because one broken step stops every lead behind it.

Run the parts in order. A broken title loses the buyer before any next step matters, and a missing next step makes lead tracing pointless.

## Part 1: score your last 10 videos

Part 1 scores the public metadata of your 10 most recent videos on four checks. Use the last 10, not your best 10, because they show what your channel does now. Give each video a pass or fail on every check below.

### 1. Titles: would a buyer type this?

A title passes when it matches something your buyer would type into YouTube or Google before buying. It fails when it only makes sense to people who already know you, or when it sells curiosity instead of an answer.

Read each title and ask: who searches this, and are they ever going to pay us? Keep the main phrase in the first half of the title and the whole title under 60 characters, so it does not get cut off in search results.

Here is a real rewrite our audit suggested for a lead-generation channel in October 2026. "Why You Can't Find Leads" became "B2B Lead Generation Mistakes: Why You Can't Find Leads".

The new title keeps the hook and adds a phrase a buyer searches. Our guide to [YouTube titles for business](/blog/youtube-titles-for-business) has more patterns that work this way.

### 2. Descriptions: do the opening lines give a reason and a link?

A description passes when its first 150 characters say what the viewer gets and include a link to the next step. Viewers see only the opening lines before they tap "more", so a link at the bottom of the description is a link most people never see.

Check three things in each description:

- The first sentence restates the video's promise in plain words a buyer would use.
- One link appears in the opening lines, pointing to the page that continues the video's topic.
- That link carries UTM tags, so a visit from it shows up in your analytics as YouTube. Part 4 covers the format.

### 3. Publishing consistency: are there gaps longer than a month?

A channel passes when its last 10 uploads have no gap longer than about 30 days. Long gaps are not a ranking penalty in themselves, but they usually mean videos ship when someone has spare time, and a channel run that way rarely builds a library around buyer questions.

Write down the upload date of each of the 10 videos and mark any gap over a month. If the gaps cluster, ask what broke: scripting, approvals or recording time.

### 4. Search packaging: chapters, playlists and tags

A video passes this check when it has chapters in the description, sits in at least one topic playlist, and uses a few tags that match the title's main phrase. Chapters matter most for business videos because a buyer can jump straight to the part that answers their question.

Shorts fail this check by design. A Short cannot carry chapters, so a library that is mostly Shorts will score low here even when everything else is strong.

Doing this by hand gets slow fast.

Ten videos times four checks is 40 judgements. It takes about 45 minutes, and by video eight most people are scoring more generously than they did at video one. You also cannot repeat it next quarter and compare like with like.

## Part 2: check every place a viewer can take the next step

Part 2 checks the next-step surfaces: every place a viewer who liked a video can move toward becoming a lead. **A next-step surface** is any spot on YouTube where a viewer can click from your video toward your business, such as a description link, pinned comment, end screen or channel page link.

Open your channel in a private browser window, as a stranger would see it, and check these five surfaces.

### Channel page: does it say who the channel is for?

The channel page passes when the banner and the first line of the About section name the buyer and the problem. "Video tutorials for clinic managers who want fewer no-shows" passes. "Welcome to our official channel" fails, because it says nothing a buyer can match to their problem.

Check that the channel page links to your website, and that the featured video is a buyer-intent video, not a company intro.

### Pinned comment: is there a next step under your top videos?

Viewers often read comments before they open the description. A pinned comment on each of your five most-watched videos should repeat the video's next step and its tracked link.

### End screens: do they point deeper into the buying journey?

An end screen passes when it sends the viewer to the video that comes next in the buyer's journey, not to whatever was uploaded most recently. A video explaining a problem should end on the video comparing solutions.

### Landing page: does it continue the video's promise?

Click the link in each description and read the page it opens. It passes when the page's headline continues the video's topic. It fails when every video sends people to the homepage, where they have to start over.

## Part 3: read YouTube Studio like a buyer, not a creator

Part 3 uses YouTube Studio to check whether the right people find your videos. The first three reports below sit in the Reach tab of YouTube Analytics, per video or for the whole channel. The same numbers creators read for growth tell a business something different.

### 1. Traffic source types: how much comes from YouTube search?

Open the traffic source types report for your channel. YouTube search is the source that best matches buyer intent, because the viewer asked for the topic. A business channel where search is a small share of views is mostly being shown to browsers, not seekers.

### 2. YouTube search terms: are buyers in the list?

The YouTube search terms report shows what viewers typed before they found your videos. Read the top 25 terms and sort them into two piles: terms a buyer would search, and terms anyone curious might search. If the buyer pile is empty, your topics are the problem, not your packaging.

### 3. CTR: judge it only after enough impressions

YouTube's own help center says half of all channels and videos have an impressions click-through rate between 2% and 10%, and advises against judging CTR before a video has a substantial number of impressions. It also notes that videos with fewer impressions often show a higher CTR, because a narrower, more loyal audience is seeing them.

Narrow audiences work in your favour. A business video shown to 800 people who all search for your exact problem can post a strong CTR and still look small.

To test a title, use the A/B test in YouTube Studio. It compares up to three titles or thumbnails, picks the winner by watch time, runs on desktop only and does not support Shorts.

### 4. Retention on your bottom-of-funnel videos

Check audience retention on the videos closest to a purchase: comparisons, pricing explainers, implementation walkthroughs. These videos usually have fewer views than your top-of-funnel content. What matters is whether the viewers who start them stay to the part where you show the product.

### 5. Geography: does your audience sit where you sell?

Open your channel analytics and look at the top geographies report. If you sell to US mid-market companies and 70% of your viewers sit in markets you do not serve, your views are real but your pipeline will not follow. In our experience, this one check explains many "views but no leads" channels.

## Part 4: trace leads back to videos

Part 4 checks whether you can name the videos that started sales conversations. None of the nine ranking audit guides we reviewed covers this step, yet it decides whether the channel survives the next budget review.

### 1. Tag every link you control

Every link in a description, pinned comment or channel page should carry UTM tags. We use this format:

```
?utm_source=youtube&utm_medium=organic_video&utm_campaign=[video-slug]&utm_content=description
```

Change `utm_content` to `pinned_comment` or `channel_page` for those placements. One campaign value per video lets you see leads per video later.

### 2. Check that the visits arrive in GA4

In GA4, filter sessions by source `youtube` and medium `organic_video`. If tagged links exist but no sessions appear after a few weeks, check the link itself: a redirect that strips query parameters is the usual cause.

### 3. Ask every new lead how they heard about you

Add a free-text "How did you hear about us?" field to your demo or contact form. Buyers often watch a video, leave, and come back days later through search or by typing your URL, and no tag survives that gap. A self-reported answer catches what tags miss.

### 4. Build a leads-per-video table

Once a month, match the tagged sessions and self-reported answers to your CRM. A simple table is enough:

## FAQ

### How do I audit my YouTube channel?

Score your last 10 videos on titles, descriptions, publishing consistency and search packaging. Then check every place a viewer can take a next step, read the Reach tab in YouTube Studio, and trace leads back to videos with tagged links and a "how did you hear about us" field. Fix the lowest-scoring area first.

### What should a YouTube channel audit checklist include for a business channel?

Four parts: public metadata (titles, descriptions, cadence, chapters), next-step surfaces (channel page, pinned comment, end screens, landing page), YouTube Studio signals (search share, search terms, CTR, retention, geography) and lead tracing (UTM links, GA4, self-reported attribution, leads per video). The scorecard above lists all 16 checks.

### How do I know if my business YouTube channel is working?

Count leads. A business channel works when you can name the videos that started sales conversations. If no lead traces back to any video after 90 days of tagged links and a "how did you hear about us" field, the channel is not working yet, whatever its view count says.

### Can ChatGPT or an AI tool audit my YouTube channel?

An AI tool can score public metadata: titles, descriptions, upload cadence and chapters. SellonTube's free channel audit does this for your last 10 videos. No public tool can see your Studio analytics or your CRM, so the Studio checks and lead tracing stay with you.

### How often should I audit my YouTube channel?

Run the metadata part after every 5 to 10 new videos and the full four-part audit once a quarter. Quarterly gives YouTube Studio enough impressions per video to read CTR and search terms properly.

### Why is my channel getting views but no leads?

Usually because the topics attract viewers who will never buy, or because the videos give buyers no next step: no link in the opening lines of the description, no pinned comment, no landing page that matches the video's promise. Parts 1 and 2 of this checklist find both.
